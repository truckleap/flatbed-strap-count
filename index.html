How Many Straps? Working the Tiedown Rules on a 40-Foot Lumber Load

Every flatbed driver gets the same question from a new hire: how many straps is enough? The federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR Part 393) answer it with two separate tests, and a load has to pass both.

Test 1: working load limit. The combined working load limit of all tiedowns must be at least half the weight of the cargo. Take a 40,000 lb lumber load. Half is 20,000 lb. Straps rated at 5,400 lb WLL each would need at least four to clear that number on paper. But hold on before you stop at four.

Test 2: the count by length. The rules also set a minimum number of tiedowns based on the length of each article. For something longer than 10 feet you need two tiedowns for the first 10 feet, plus one more for each additional 10 feet or part of it. A 40-foot bundle that is not blocked at the front needs two for the first 10 feet and three more for the remaining 30: five tiedowns. If you are hauling two 20-foot stacks side by side, count each stack on its own.

Putting it together. Five 5,400 lb straps give 27,000 lb of WLL, which beats 20,000 lb, and five meets the count. So five is the legal minimum here. Most experienced drivers add one or two because a bundle shifts, edge protectors slip, and an inspector is looking at the worst strap, not the best.

Two traps that cost money.

  • A strap with a missing or unreadable tag is rated at the default value in the rules, which is usually lower than the rating you paid for. Replace them before they cost you a citation.
  • Spreading the load badly can pass securement and still fail the scale. Before you chain or strap anything, check your axle split before you scale.

Why dispatch cares. Securement takes time, edge protection, and sometimes tarps. A load that needs seven straps and a tarp should not pay like a load that needs four. We talk this through with carriers before booking, because people who book open-deck freight should know what it takes to tie it down.

Always check the current rule text and your state's guidance; this post is a worked example, not legal advice.
